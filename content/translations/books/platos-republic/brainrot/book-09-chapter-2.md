# Book 9, Chapter 2: Comparing Lives - Just vs Unjust (The Ultimate Life Tier List)

## The Enslaved Soul Analysis

"inevitably."

"and would you say that the soul of such a one is the soul of a freeman, or of a slave?"

"they have the soul of a slave, in my opinion."

"and the state which is enslaved under a tyrant is utterly incapable of acting voluntarily?"

"utterly incapable."

"and also the soul which is under a tyrant (i'm speaking of the soul taken as a whole) is least capable of doing what she desires; there's a gadfly which goads her, and she's full of trouble and remorse?"

"certainly."

## The Poverty and Fear Analysis

"and is the city which is under a tyrant rich or poor?"

"poor."

"and the tyrannical soul must be always poor and insatiable?"

"true."

"and must not such a state and such a person be always full of fear?"

"yes, indeed."

"is there any state in which you'll find more of lamentation and sorrow and groaning and pain?"

"certainly not."

"and is there any person in whom you'll find more of this sort of misery than in the tyrannical person, who's in a fury of passions and desires?"

"impossible."

## The Misery Ranking Begins

"reflecting upon these and similar evils, you held the tyrannical state to be the most miserable of states?"

"and i was right," he said.

"certainly," i said. "and when you see the same evils in the tyrannical person, what do you say of them?"

"i say that they're by far the most miserable of all people."

"there," i said, "i think that you're beginning to go wrong."

"what do you mean?"

"i don't think that they've as yet reached the utmost extreme of misery."

"then who's more miserable?"

"one of whom i'm about to speak."

"who's that?"

## The Public vs Private Tyrant Problem

"they who are of a tyrannical nature, and instead of leading a private life have been cursed with the further misfortune of being a public tyrant."

"from what's been said, i gather that you're right."

"yes," i replied, "but in this high argument you should be a little more certain, and shouldn't conjecture only; cause of all questions, this respecting good and evil is the greatest."

"very true," he said.

"let me then offer you an illustration, which may, i think, throw a light upon this subject."

"what's your illustration?"

## The Slave Owner Analogy (Absolutely Based)

"the case of rich individuals in cities who possess many slaves: from them you may form an idea of the tyrant's condition, cause they both have slaves; the only difference is that they have more slaves."

"yes, that's the difference."

"you know that they live securely and have nothing to apprehend from their servants?"

"what should they fear?"

"nothing. but do you observe the reason of this?"

"yes; the reason is, that the whole city is leagued together for the protection of each individual."

## The Wilderness Experiment

"very true," i said. "but imagine one of these owners, the master say of some fifty slaves, together with their family and property and slaves, carried off by a god into the wilderness, where there are no freemen to help them—won't they be in an agony of fear lest they and their wife and children should be put to death by their slaves?"

"yes," he said, "they'll be in the utmost fear."

"the time has arrived when they'll be compelled to flatter divers of their slaves, and make many promises to them of freedom and other things, much against their will—they'll have to cajole their own servants."

"yes," he said, "that'll be the only way of saving themselves."

"and suppose the same god, who carried them away, to surround them with neighbors who won't suffer one person to be the master of another, and who, if they could catch the offender, would take their life?"

"their case will be still worse, if you suppose them to be everywhere surrounded and watched by enemies."

## The Tyrant's Prison

"and isn't this the sort of prison in which the tyrant will be bound—they who being by nature such as we've described, are full of all sorts of fears and lusts? their soul is dainty and greedy, and yet alone, of all people in the city, they're never allowed to go on a journey, or to see the things which other freemen desire to see, but they live in their hole like a woman hidden in the house, and are jealous of any other citizen who goes into foreign parts and sees anything of interest."

"very true," he said.

## The Ultimate Misery Comparison

"and amid evils such as these won't they who are ill-governed in their own person—the tyrannical person, i mean—whom you just now decided to be the most miserable of all—won't they be yet more miserable when, instead of leading a private life, they're constrained by fortune to be a public tyrant? they have to be master of others when they're not master of themselves: they're like a diseased or paralytic person who's compelled to pass their life, not in retirement, but fighting and combating with other people."

"yes," he said, "the similitude is most exact."

"isn't their case utterly miserable? and doesn't the actual tyrant lead a worse life than they whose life you determined to be the worst?"

"certainly."

## The Real Slave Analysis (Actually Fire)

"they who are the real tyrant, whatever people may think, are the real slave, and are obliged to practice the greatest adulation and servility, and to be the flatterer of the vilest of humankind. they have desires which they're utterly unable to satisfy, and have more wants than anyone, and are truly poor, if you know how to inspect the whole soul of them: all their life long they're beset with fear and are full of convulsions and distractions, even as the state which they resemble: and surely the resemblance holds?"

"very true," he said.

"moreover, as we were saying before, they grow worse from having power: they become and are of necessity more jealous, more faithless, more unjust, more friendless, more impious, than they were at first; they're the purveyor and cherisher of every sort of vice, and the consequence is that they're supremely miserable, and that they make everybody else as miserable as themselves."

"no person of any sense will dispute your words."

## The Official Life Tier List (Peak Content)

"come then," i said, "and as the general umpire in theatrical contests proclaims the result, do you also decide who in your opinion is first in the scale of happiness, and who second, and in what order the others follow: there are five of them in all—they're the royal, timocratical, oligarchical, democratical, tyrannical."

"the decision will be easily given," he replied; "they shall be choruses coming on the stage, and i must judge them in the order in which they enter, by the criterion of virtue and vice, happiness and misery."

"need we hire a herald, or shall i announce, that the son of ariston (the best) has decided that the best and justest is also the happiest, and that this is they who are the most royal person and king over themselves; and that the worst and most unjust person is also the most miserable, and that this is they who being the greatest tyrant of themselves are also the greatest tyrant of their state?"

"make the proclamation yourself," he said.

"and shall i add, 'whether seen or unseen by gods and people'?"

"let the words be added."

## The Second Proof Setup

"then this," i said, "will be our first proof; and there's another, which may also have some weight."

"what's that?"

"the second proof is derived from the nature of the soul: seeing that the individual soul, like the state, has been divided by us into three principles, the division may, i think, furnish a new demonstration."

"of what nature?"

"it seems to me that to these three principles three pleasures correspond; also three desires and governing powers."

"how do you mean?" he said.

## The Three Soul Parts Classification

"there's one principle with which, as we were saying, a person learns, another with which they're angry; the third, having many forms, has no special name, but is denoted by the general term appetitive, from the extraordinary strength and vehemence of the desires of eating and drinking and the other sensual appetites which are the main elements of it; also money-loving, because such desires are generally satisfied by the help of money."

"that's true," he said.

"if we were to say that the loves and pleasures of this third part were concerned with gain, we should then be able to fall back on a single notion; and might truly and intelligibly describe this part of the soul as loving gain or money."

"i agree with you."

## The Competitive Spirit Analysis

"again, isn't the passionate element wholly set on ruling and conquering and getting fame?"

"true."

"suppose we call it the contentious or ambitious—would the term be suitable?"

"extremely suitable."

"on the other hand, everyone sees that the principle of knowledge is wholly directed to the truth, and cares less than either of the others for gain or fame."

"far less."

"'lover of wisdom,' 'lover of knowledge,' are titles which we may fitly apply to that part of the soul?"

"certainly."

## The Three Character Types

"one principle prevails in the souls of one class of people, another in others, as may happen?"

"yes."

"then we may begin by assuming that there are three classes of people—lovers of wisdom, lovers of honor, lovers of gain?"

"exactly."

"and there are three kinds of pleasure, which are their several objects?"

"very true."

## The Subjective Bias Problem

"now, if you examine the three classes of people, and ask of them in turn which of their lives is pleasantest, each will be found praising their own and depreciating that of others: the money-maker will contrast the vanity of honor or of learning if they bring no money with the solid advantages of gold and silver?"

"true," he said.

"and the lover of honor—what will be their opinion? won't they think that the pleasure of riches is vulgar, while the pleasure of learning, if it brings no distinction, is all smoke and nonsense to them?"

"very true."

"and are we to suppose," i said, "that the philosopher sets any value on other pleasures in comparison with the pleasure of knowing the truth, and in that pursuit abiding, ever learning, not so far indeed from the heaven of pleasure? don't they call the other pleasures necessary, under the idea that if there were no necessity for them, they would rather not have them?"

"there can be no doubt of that," he replied.

## The Judging Criteria Problem

"since, then, the pleasures of each class and the life of each are in dispute, and the question isn't which life is more or less honorable, or better or worse, but which is the more pleasant or painless—how shall we know who speaks truly?"

"i can't myself tell," he said.

"well, but what ought to be the criterion? is any better than experience and wisdom and reason?"

"there can't be a better," he said.

## The Experience Test (Absolutely Goated)

"then," i said, "reflect. of the three individuals, which has the greatest experience of all the pleasures which we enumerated? has the lover of gain, in learning the nature of essential truth, greater experience of the pleasure of knowledge than the philosopher has of the pleasure of gain?"

"the philosopher," he replied, "has greatly the advantage; cause they have of necessity always known the taste of the other pleasures from their childhood upwards: but the lover of gain in all their experience has not of necessity tasted—or, i should rather say, even had they desired, could hardly have tasted—the sweetness of learning and knowing truth."

"then the lover of wisdom has a great advantage over the lover of gain, cause they have a double experience?"

"yes, very great."

## The Honor vs Wisdom Test

"again, have they greater experience of the pleasures of honor, or the lover of honor of the pleasures of wisdom?"

"nay," he said, "all three are honored in proportion as they attain their object; cause the rich person and the brave person and the wise person alike have their crowd of admirers, and as they all receive honor they all have experience of the pleasures of honor; but the delight which is to be found in the knowledge of true being is known to the philosopher only."

"their experience, then, will enable them to judge better than anyone?"

"far better."

"and they're the only one who has wisdom as well as experience?"

"certainly."

## The Reasoning Faculty Test

"further, the very faculty which is the instrument of judgment isn't possessed by the covetous or ambitious person, but only by the philosopher?"

"what faculty?"

"reason, with whom, as we were saying, the decision ought to rest."

"yes."

"and reasoning is peculiarly their instrument?"

"certainly."

"if wealth and gain were the criterion, then the praise or blame of the lover of gain would surely be the most trustworthy?"

"assuredly."

"or if honor or victory or courage, in that case the judgment of the ambitious or pugnacious would be the truest?"

"clearly."

"but since experience and wisdom and reason are the judges—"

"the only inference possible," he replied, "is that pleasures which are approved by the lover of wisdom and reason are the truest."

## The Final Ranking

"and so we arrive at the result, that the pleasure of the intelligent part of the soul is the pleasantest of the three, and that they of us in whom this is the ruling principle have the pleasantest life."

"unquestionably," he said, "the wise person speaks with authority when they approve of their own life."

"and what does the judge affirm to be the life which is next, and the pleasure which is next?"

"clearly that of the soldier and lover of honor; who's nearer to themselves than the money-maker."

"last comes the lover of gain?"

"very true," he said.

## The Victory Declaration

"twice in succession, then, has the just person overthrown the unjust in this conflict; and now comes the third trial, which is dedicated to olympian zeus the savior: a sage whispers in my ear that no pleasure except that of the wise is quite true and pure—all others are a shadow only; and surely this will prove the greatest and most decisive of falls?"

"yes, the greatest; but will you explain yourself?"

"i'll work out the subject and you shall answer my questions."

"proceed."

## The Third Proof: True vs False Pleasures

"say, then, isn't pleasure opposed to pain?"

"true."

"and there's a neutral state which is neither pleasure nor pain?"

"there is."

"a state which is intermediate, and a sort of repose of the soul about either—that's what you mean?"

"yes."

"you remember what people say when they're sick?"

"what do they say?"

"that after all nothing is pleasanter than health. but then they never knew this to be the greatest of pleasures until they were ill."
