# Book 8, Chapter 2: Oligarchy - Rule by Wealth (The Grindset Government Arc)

## The Next Government Type

"we have fr fr."

"next, let's look at another person who, as aeschylus says, 'is set over against another state;' or rather, as our plan requires, begin with the state."

"by all means."

"i believe that oligarchy follows next in order."

"and what manner of government do you term oligarchy?"

"a government resting on a valuation of property, where the rich have power and the poor person is deprived of it."

"i understand," he replied.

## The Timocracy → Oligarchy Pipeline

"ought i not to begin by describing how the change from timocracy to oligarchy arises?"

"yes."

"well," i said, "no eyes are required in order to see how the one passes into the other."

"how?"

## The Money Corruption Arc

"the accumulation of gold in the treasury of private individuals is the ruin of timocracy; they invent illegal modes of expenditure; cause what do they or their wives care about the law?"

"yes, indeed."

"and then one, seeing another grow rich, seeks to rival him, and thus the great mass of the citizens become lovers of money."

"likely enough."

"and so they grow richer and richer, and the more they think of making a fortune the less they think of virtue; cause when riches and virtue are placed together in the scales of the balance, the one always rises as the other falls."

"true."

"and in proportion as riches and rich people are honored in the state, virtue and the virtuous are dishonored."

"clearly."

"and what is honored is cultivated, and that which has no honor is neglected."

"that's obvious."

"and so at last, instead of loving contention and glory, people become lovers of trade and money; they honor and look up to the rich person, and make a ruler of them, and dishonor the poor person."

"they do so."

## The Wealth Requirement System

"they next proceed to make a law which fixes a sum of money as the qualification of citizenship; the sum is higher in one place and lower in another, as the oligarchy is more or less exclusive; and they allow no one whose property falls below the amount fixed to have any share in the government. these changes in the constitution they effect by force of arms, if intimidation hasn't already done their work."

"very true."

"and this, speaking generally, is the way oligarchy is established."

"yes," he said; "but what are the characteristics of this form of government, and what are the defects we were talking about?"

## The Pilot Analogy (Peak Socratic Trolling)

"first of all," i said, "consider the nature of the qualification. just think what would happen if pilots were chosen according to their property, and a poor person were refused permission to steer, even though they were a better pilot?"

"you mean they would shipwreck?"

"yes; and isn't this true of the government of anything?"

"i should imagine so."

"except a city?—or would you include a city?"

"nay," he said, "the case of a city is the strongest of all, inasmuch as the rule of a city is the greatest and most difficult of all."

"this, then, will be the first great defect of oligarchy?"

"clearly."

## The Two-State Problem

"and here's another defect which is quite as bad."

"what defect?"

"the inevitable division: such a state isn't one, but two states, the one of poor, the other of rich people; and they're living on the same spot and always conspiring against one another."

"that, surely, is at least as bad."

## The Military Problems (They Can't Fight)

"another scuffed feature is that, for a like reason, they're incapable of carrying on any war. either they arm the multitude, and then they're more afraid of them than of the enemy; or, if they don't call them out in the hour of battle, they're oligarchs indeed, few to fight as they are few to rule. and at the same time their fondness for money makes them unwilling to pay taxes."

"how scuffed!"

"and, as we said before, under such a constitution the same persons have too many callings—they're farmers, tradespeople, warriors, all in one. does that look well?"

"anything but well."

## The Ultimate Problem: Economic Refugees

"there's another evil which is, perhaps, the greatest of all, and to which this state first begins to be liable."

"what evil?"

"a person may sell all that they have, and another may acquire their property; yet after the sale they may dwell in the city of which they're no longer a part, being neither trader, nor artisan, nor horseman, nor hoplite, but only a poor, helpless creature."

"yes, that's an evil which also first begins in this state."

"the evil certainly isn't prevented there; cause oligarchies have both the extremes of great wealth and utter poverty."

"true."

## The Spendthrift Analysis

"but think again: in their wealthy days, while they were spending their money, was a person of this sort a whit more good to the state for the purposes of citizenship? or did they only seem to be a member of the ruling body, although in truth they were neither ruler nor subject, but just a spendthrift?"

"as you say, they seemed to be a ruler, but were only a spendthrift."

## The Drone Metaphor (Absolute Banger)

"may we not say that this is the drone in the house who's like the drone in the honeycomb, and that the one is the plague of the city as the other is of the hive?"

"just so, socrates."

"and god has made the flying drones, adeimantus, all without stings, whereas of the walking drones he's made some without stings but others have dreadful stings; of the stingless class are those who in their old age end as paupers; of the stingers come all the criminal class, as they're termed."

"most true," he said.

"clearly then, whenever you see paupers in a state, somewhere in that neighborhood there are hidden away thieves, and pickpockets and robbers of temples, and all sorts of malefactors."

"clearly."

"well," i said, "and in oligarchical states do you not find paupers?"

"yes," he said; "nearly everybody is a pauper who isn't a ruler."

"and may we be so bold as to affirm that there are also many criminals to be found in them, rogues who have stings, and whom the authorities are careful to restrain by force?"

"certainly, we may be so bold."

"the existence of such persons is to be attributed to want of education, ill-training, and an evil constitution of the state?"

"true."

"such, then, is the form and such are the evils of oligarchy; and there may be many other evils."

"very likely."

## The Oligarch Individual Origin Story

"then oligarchy, or the form of government in which the rulers are elected for their wealth, may now be dismissed. let's next proceed to consider the nature and origin of the individual who answers to this state."

"by all means."

"doesn't the timocratic person change into the oligarchical on this wise?"

"how?"

## The Traumatized Rich Kid Arc

"a time arrives when the representative of timocracy has a child: at first they begin by emulating their parent and walking in their footsteps, but presently they see them of a sudden foundering against the state as upon a sunken reef, and they and all that they have is lost; they may have been a general or some other high officer who's brought to trial under a prejudice raised by informers, and either put to death, or exiled, or deprived of the privileges of a citizen, and all their property taken from them."

"nothing more likely."

"and the child has seen and known all this—they're a ruined person, and their fear has taught them to knock ambition and passion headfirst from their bosom's throne; humbled by poverty they take to money-making and by mean and miserly savings and hard work get a fortune together. isn't such a one likely to seat the concupiscent and covetous element on the vacant throne and to suffer it to play the great king within them, girt with tiara and chain and scimitar?"

"most true," he replied.

## The Internal Monarchy of Greed

"and when they've made reason and spirit sit down on the ground obediently on either side of their sovereign, and taught them to know their place, they compel the one to think only of how lesser sums may be turned into larger ones, and won't allow the other to worship and admire anything but riches and rich people, or to be ambitious of anything so much as the acquisition of wealth and the means of acquiring it."

"of all changes," he said, "there's none so speedy or so sure as the conversion of the ambitious youth into the avaricious one."

"and the avaricious," i said, "is the oligarchical youth?"

"yes," he said; "at any rate the individual out of whom they came is like the state out of which oligarchy came."

## The Oligarch Character Profile

"let's then consider whether there's any likeness between them."

"very good."

"first, then, they resemble one another in the value which they set upon wealth?"

"certainly."

"also in their stingy, laborious character; the individual only satisfies their necessary appetites, and confines their expenditure to them; their other desires they subdue, under the idea that they're unprofitable."

"true."

"they're a shabby fellow, who saves something out of everything and makes a purse for themselves; and this is the sort of person whom the vulgar applaud. aren't they a true image of the state which they represent?"

"they appear to me to be so; at any rate money is highly valued by them as well as by the state."

## The Cultural Emptiness

"you see that they're not a person of cultivation," i said.

"i imagine not," he said; "had they been educated they would never have made a blind god director of their chorus, or given him chief honor."

"excellent!" i said. "yet consider: must we not further admit that owing to this want of cultivation there'll be found in them drone-like desires as of pauper and rogue, which are forcibly kept down by their general habit of life?"

"true."

## Where The Mask Comes Off

"do you know where you'll have to look if you want to discover their rogueries?"

"where must i look?"

"you should see them where they have some great opportunity of acting dishonestly, as in the guardianship of an orphan."

"aye."

"it'll be clear enough then that in their ordinary dealings which give them a reputation for honesty they coerce their bad passions by an enforced virtue; not making them see that they're wrong, or taming them by reason, but by necessity and fear constraining them, and because they tremble for their possessions."

"to be sure."

"yes, indeed, bestie, but you'll find that the natural desires of the drone commonly exist in them all the same whenever they have to spend what's not their own."

"yes, and they'll be strong in them too."

## The Internal Conflict

"the person, then, will be at war with themselves; they'll be two people, and not one; but, in general, their better desires will be found to prevail over their inferior ones."

"true."

"for these reasons such a one will be more respectable than most people; yet the true virtue of a unanimous and harmonious soul will flee far away and never come near them."

"i should expect so."

## The Competition Problem

"and surely, the miser individually will be an absolute mid competitor in a state for any prize of victory, or other object of honorable ambition; they won't spend their money in the contest for glory; so afraid are they of awakening their expensive appetites and inviting them to help and join in the struggle; in true oligarchical fashion they fight with a small part only of their resources, and the result commonly is that they lose the prize and save their money."

"very true."

"can we any longer doubt, then, that the miser and money-maker answers to the oligarchical state?"

"there can be no doubt."

## The Democracy Setup

"next comes democracy; of this the origin and nature have still to be considered by us; and then we'll inquire into the ways of the democratic person, and bring them up for judgment."

"that," he said, "is our method."

"well," i said, "and how does the change from oligarchy into democracy arise? isn't it on this wise?—the good at which such a state aims is to become as rich as possible, a desire which is insatiable?"

"what then?"

## The Oligarch's Greed Problem

"the rulers, being aware that their power rests upon their wealth, refuse to curtail by law the extravagance of the spendthrift youth because they gain by their ruin; they take interest from them and buy up their estates and thus increase their own wealth and importance?"

"to be sure."

"there can be no doubt that the love of wealth and the spirit of moderation can't exist together in citizens of the same state to any considerable extent; one or the other will be disregarded."

"that's tolerably clear."

"and in oligarchical states, from the general spread of carelessness and extravagance, people of good family have often been reduced to beggary?"

"yes, often."

## The Revolutionary Class Formation

"and still they remain in the city; there they are, ready to sting and fully armed, and some of them owe money, some have forfeited their citizenship; a third class are in both predicaments; and they hate and conspire against those who've got their property, and against everybody else, and are eager for revolution."

"that's true."

"on the other hand, the people of business, stooping as they walk, and pretending not even to see those whom they've already ruined, insert their sting—that is, their money—into someone else who's not on their guard against them, and recover the parent sum many times over multiplied into a family of children: and so they make drone and pauper to abound in the state."

"yes," he said, "there are plenty of them—that's certain."

## The Fire That Can't Be Put Out

"the evil blazes up like a fire; and they won't extinguish it, either by restricting a person's use of their own property, or by another remedy:"

"what other?"

"one which is the next best, and has the advantage of compelling the citizens to look to their characters:—let there be a general rule that everyone shall enter into voluntary contracts at their own risk, and there'll be less of this scandalous money-making, and the evils of which we were speaking will be greatly lessened in the state."

"yes, they'll be greatly lessened."

## The Ruling Class Decay

"at present the governors, induced by the motives which i've named, treat their subjects badly; while they and their adherents, especially the young people of the governing class, are habituated to lead a life of luxury and idleness both of body and mind; they do nothing, and are incapable of resisting either pleasure or pain."

"very true."

"they themselves care only for making money, and are as indifferent as the pauper to the cultivation of virtue."

"yes, quite as indifferent."

## The Class Confrontation

"such is the state of affairs which prevails among them. and often rulers and their subjects may come in one another's way, whether on a journey or on some other occasion of meeting, on a pilgrimage or a march, as fellow-soldiers or fellow-sailors; aye and they may observe the behavior of each other in the very moment of danger—cause where danger is, there's no fear that the poor will be despised by the rich—and very likely the wiry sunburnt poor person may be placed in battle at the side of a wealthy one who's never spoiled their complexion and has plenty of superfluous flesh—when they see such a one puffing and at their wits' end, how can they avoid drawing the conclusion that people like them are only rich because no one has the courage to despoil them?"
