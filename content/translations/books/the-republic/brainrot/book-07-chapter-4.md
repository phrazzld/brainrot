"that's an absolutely based take," he said.

"do you remember that the kids too were supposed to be taken to see battle on horseback; and that if there was no danger they were to be brought up close and, like young hounds, get a taste of blood?"

"yes, i remember."

"the same practice can be followed," i said, "in all these things—labors, lessons, dangers—and whoever's most at home in all of them should be enrolled in a select squad."

"at what age?"

"at the age when the necessary gymnastics are over: the period of two or three years spent in this kind of training is useless for any other purpose; cause sleep and exercise are absolutely not conducive to learning; and the trial of who's first in gymnastic exercises is one of the most important tests our youth face."

"certainly," he replied.

"after that time those who are selected from the twenty-year-old class will be promoted to higher clout, and the sciences they learned without any order in their early education will now be brought together, and they'll be able to see the natural relationship between them and to true being."

"yes," he said, "that's the only kind of knowledge that takes lasting root."

"yes," i said; "and the capacity for such knowledge is the ultimate criterion of dialectical talent: the comprehensive mind is always the dialectical one."

"i agree with you," he said.

"these," i said, "are the points you must consider; and those who have the most of this comprehension, and who are most solid in their learning, and in their military and other appointed duties, when they've reached the age of thirty have to be chosen by you out of the select class, and elevated to higher status; and you'll have to test them with the help of dialectic, to learn which of them can give up using sight and the other senses, and in company with truth reach absolute being: and here, bestie, absolute caution is required."

"why absolute caution?"

"don't you notice," i said, "how massive is the damage dialectic has caused?"

"what damage?" he said.

"the students of the art become filled with lawlessness."

"absolutely true," he said.

"do you think there's anything particularly unnatural or inexcusable in their case? or will you cut them some slack?"

"in what way cut them slack?"

"i want you," i said, "as a parallel, to imagine a hypothetical son who's brought up in serious wealth; he's one of a huge and numerous family, and has many people kissing his ass. when he grows up to adulthood, he learns that his supposed parents aren't his real ones; but who the real ones are he can't discover. can you guess how he'll likely behave towards his flatterers and his supposed parents, first during the period when he's ignorant of the false relation, and then again when he knows? or should i guess for you?"

"if you please."

"then i'd say that while he's ignorant of the truth he'll be likely to honor his father and mother and his supposed relatives more than the flatterers; he'll be less inclined to neglect them when in need, or to do or say anything against them; and he'll be less willing to disobey them in any important matter."

"he will."

"but when he's made the discovery, i'd imagine he'd diminish his honor and regard for them, and would become more devoted to the flatterers; their influence over him would massively increase; he'd now live according to their ways, and openly associate with them, and, unless he had an unusually good disposition, he wouldn't trouble himself anymore about his supposed parents or other relatives."

"well, all that's very likely. but how does this image apply to the disciples of philosophy?"

"in this way: you know there are certain principles about justice and honor that were taught to us in childhood, and under their parental authority we were brought up, obeying and honoring them."

"that's true."

"there are also opposite maxims and habits of pleasure that flatter and attract the soul, but don't influence those of us who have any sense of right, and we continue to obey and honor our fathers' maxims."

"true."

"now, when someone's in this state, and the questioning spirit asks what's fair or honorable, and they answer as the legislator taught them, and then arguments many and diverse absolutely destroy their words, until they're driven into believing that nothing is honorable any more than dishonorable, or just and good any more than the reverse, and so with all the notions they most valued, do you think they'll still honor and obey them as before?"

"impossible."

"and when they stop thinking these ideas honorable and natural as before, and fail to discover the true ones, can we expect them to pursue any life other than one that flatters their desires?"

"they can't."

"and from being a keeper of the law they become a breaker of it?"

"unquestionably."

"now all this is very natural in philosophy students such as i've described, and also, as i was just saying, most understandable."

"yes," he said; "and, i might add, kind of sad."

"therefore, so your feelings won't be moved to pity about our citizens who are now thirty years old, absolute care must be taken in introducing them to dialectic."

"certainly."

"there's a danger they might taste the sweet delight too early; cause youngsters, as you might've observed, when they first get the taste in their mouths, argue for fun, and are always contradicting and refuting others in imitation of those who refute them; like puppies, they love pulling and tearing at all who come near them."

"yes," he said, "there's literally nothing they like better."

"and when they've made many conquests and received defeats at the hands of many, they violently and quickly get into a way of not believing anything they believed before, and hence, not only they, but philosophy and all that relates to it tends to get a bad name with the rest of the world."

"too true," he said.

"but when someone starts to get older, they'll no longer be guilty of such absolute chaos; they'll imitate the dialectician who's seeking truth, not the troll who's contradicting for fun; and the greater moderation of their character will increase rather than diminish the honor of the pursuit."

"very true," he said.

"and didn't we make special provision for this when we said the disciples of philosophy were to be orderly and solid, not, as now, any random wannabe or intruder?"

"very true."

"suppose," i said, "the study of philosophy takes the place of gymnastics and continues diligently and earnestly and exclusively for twice the number of years spent in bodily exercise—will that be enough?"

"would you say six or four years?" he asked.

"say five years," i replied; "at the end of that time they must be sent down again into the cave and forced to hold any military or other office young people are qualified for: this way they'll get their life experience, and there'll be an opportunity to test whether, when they're pulled in all directions by temptation, they'll stand firm or fold."

"and how long is this stage of their lives to last?"

"fifteen years," i answered; "and when they've reached fifty years of age, then let those who still survive and have distinguished themselves in every action of their lives and in every branch of knowledge come finally to their ultimate goal: the time has now arrived when they must raise the eye of the soul to the universal light that lights up all things, and see the absolute good; cause that's the pattern according to which they're to order the state and the lives of individuals, and the remainder of their own lives too; making philosophy their chief pursuit, but when their turn comes, grinding also at politics and ruling for the public good, not as though they were performing some heroic action, but simply as a duty; and when they've brought up in each generation others like themselves and left them in their place to be governors of the state, then they'll depart to the islands of the blessed and dwell there; and the city will give them public memorials and sacrifices and honor them, if the pythian oracle consents, as demigods, but if not, as in any case blessed and divine."

"you're a sculptor, socrates, and have made statues of our governors absolutely flawless in beauty."

"yes," i said, "glaucon, and of our governesses too; cause you mustn't suppose that what i've been saying applies only to men and not to women as far as their natures can go."

"there you're right," he said, "since we've made them share in all things like the men."

"well," i said, "and you'd agree (wouldn't you?) that what's been said about the state and the government isn't just some fantasy, and although difficult not impossible, but only possible in the way that's been supposed; that is, when the true philosopher kings are born in a state, one or more of them, despising the honors of this present world which they consider mid and worthless, valuing above all things what's right and the honor that springs from right, and regarding justice as the greatest and most necessary of all things, whose servants they are, and whose principles will be exalted by them when they set their own city in order?"

"how will they proceed?"

"they'll begin by sending out into the country all the inhabitants of the city who are more than ten years old, and will take possession of their children, who'll be unaffected by the habits of their parents; these they'll train in their own habits and laws, i mean in the laws we've given them: and this way the state and constitution we were talking about will soonest and most easily attain happiness, and the nation that has such a constitution will gain most."

"yes, that'll be the best way. and i think, socrates, that you've very well described how, if ever, such a constitution might come into being."

"enough then of the perfect state, and of the person who bears its image—there's no difficulty in seeing how we'll describe them."

"there's no difficulty," he replied; "and i agree with you in thinking that nothing more needs to be said."
