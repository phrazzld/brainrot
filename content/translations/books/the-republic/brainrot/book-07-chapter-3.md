"you mean," i said, "those people who absolutely torture the strings and literally rack them on the pegs of the instrument: i could go off about the metaphor and talk about the beatdowns the plectrum gives, and make accusations against the strings for being either too slow or too eager to sound; but that would be tedious, so i'll just say that these aren't the people i mean, and i'm talking about the pythagoreans, who i was just proposing to ask about harmony. cause they're also taking l's, just like the astronomers; they investigate the numbers of the harmonies that are heard, but they never reach the actual problems—basically, they never get to the natural harmonies of number, or think about why some numbers are harmonious and others aren't."

"that," he said, "is some absolute god-tier knowledge."

"a thing," i replied, "which i'd rather call useful; that is, if pursued with a view to the beautiful and good; but if chased in any other spirit, it's useless."

"very true," he said.

"now, when all these studies reach the point of interconnection and linking with each other, and come to be considered in their mutual relationships, then, i think, but not till then, will pursuing them have value for our goals; otherwise there's no profit in them."

"i suspect so; but you're talking about an absolutely massive grind, socrates."

"what do you mean?" i said; "the prelude or what? don't you know that all this is just the prelude to the actual banger we have to learn? cause you surely wouldn't consider the skilled mathematician a dialectician?"

"absolutely not," he said; "i've barely ever known a mathematician who could actually reason."

"but do you imagine that people who can't give and take a reason will have the knowledge we require of them?"

"that can't be assumed either."

"and so, glaucon," i said, "we've finally arrived at the hymn of dialectic. this is that banger which is of the intellect only, but which the faculty of sight will still be found to imitate; cause sight, as you might remember, was imagined by us after a while to see the real animals and stars, and finally the sun itself. and so with dialectic; when someone starts on the discovery of the absolute by the light of reason only, without any help from the senses, and grinds until by pure intelligence they arrive at the perception of the absolute good, they finally find themselves at the end of the intellectual world, just like in the case of sight at the end of the visible."

"exactly," he said.

"then this is the progress you call dialectic?"

"true."

"but the release of the prisoners from chains, and their translation from the shadows to the images and to the light, and the ascent from the underground cave to the sun, while in his presence they're desperately trying to look at animals and plants and the sunlight, but can only perceive even with their weak eyes the images in the water (which are divine), and are the shadows of true existence (not shadows of images cast by firelight, which compared with the sun is just an image)—this power of elevating the highest principle in the soul to contemplating what's best in existence, which we can compare to raising that faculty which is literally the light of the body to seeing what's brightest in the material and visible world—this power is given, as i was saying, by all that study and pursuit of the arts we've been describing."

"i agree with what you're saying," he replied, "which might be hard to believe, yet, from another angle, is even harder to deny. this, however, isn't a topic to be treated just in passing, but will have to be discussed again and again. and so, whether our conclusion is true or false, let's assume all this, and proceed immediately from the prelude or preamble to the chief strain" (a play on the greek word, which means both 'law' and 'strain'), "and describe that similarly. so tell me, what's the nature and what are the divisions of dialectic, and what are the paths that lead there; cause these paths will also lead to our final rest."

"dear glaucon," i said, "you won't be able to follow me here, though i'd do my absolute best, and you'd see not just an image but the absolute truth, according to my take. whether what i told you would or wouldn't have been reality i can't venture to say; but you would've seen something like reality; of that i'm confident."

"no doubt," he replied.

"but i must also remind you that the power of dialectic alone can reveal this, and only to someone who's a disciple of the previous sciences."

"of that claim you can be as confident as the last."

"and surely no one will argue there's any other method of comprehending by any regular process all true existence or figuring out what each thing is in its own nature; cause the arts in general are concerned with the desires or opinions of people, or are cultivated for production and construction, or for preserving such productions and constructions; and as for the mathematical sciences which, as we were saying, have some grasp of true being—geometry and the like—they only dream about being, but can never see the waking reality as long as they leave the hypotheses they use unexamined, and can't give an account of them. cause when someone doesn't know their own first principle, and when the conclusion and intermediate steps are also built out of they don't know what, how can they imagine that such a construction of convention can ever become science?"

"impossible," he said.

"then dialectic, and dialectic alone, goes directly to the first principle and is the only science that does away with hypotheses to make her ground secure; the eye of the soul, which is literally buried in some barbaric swamp, is by her gentle aid lifted upwards; and she uses as assistants and helpers in the work of conversion, the sciences we've been discussing. custom calls them sciences, but they should have some other name, implying more clarity than opinion and less clarity than science: and this, in our previous sketch, was called understanding. but why should we beef about names when we have realities of such importance to consider?"

"why indeed," he said, "when any name will work that expresses the thought of the mind clearly?"

"anyway, we're satisfied, as before, to have four divisions; two for intellect and two for opinion, and to call the first division science, the second understanding, the third belief, and the fourth perception of shadows, opinion being concerned with becoming, and intellect with being; and so to make a proportion:

as being is to becoming, so is pure intellect to opinion. and as intellect is to opinion, so is science to belief, and understanding to the perception of shadows.

but let's postpone the further correlation and subdivision of the subjects of opinion and intellect, cause it'll be a massive investigation, way longer than this has been."

"as far as i understand," he said, "i agree."

"and do you also agree," i said, "in describing the dialectician as someone who gets a conception of the essence of each thing? and whoever doesn't have and therefore can't share this conception, to whatever degree they fail, can in that degree also be said to fail in intelligence? will you admit that much?"

"yes," he said; "how can i deny it?"

"and you'd say the same about the conception of the good? until someone can abstract and define rationally the idea of good, and unless they can speedrun through all objections, ready to disprove them, not by appeals to opinion but to absolute truth, never folding at any step of the argument—unless they can do all this, you'd say they know neither the idea of good nor any other good; they grasp only a shadow, if anything at all, which is given by opinion and not by science;—dreaming and sleeping through this life, before they're properly awake here, they arrive at the underworld, and get their final gg."

"in all that i'd absolutely agree with you."

"and surely you wouldn't want the children of your ideal state, who you're raising and educating—if the ideal ever becomes reality—you wouldn't allow the future rulers to be like posts" (literally 'lines,' probably the starting point of a race course), "having no reason in them, and yet to be set in authority over the most important matters?"

"definitely not."

"then you'll make a law that they must have the kind of education that'll enable them to achieve the greatest skill in asking and answering questions?"

"yes," he said, "you and i together will make it."

"dialectic, then, as you'll agree, is the capstone of the sciences, and is set over them; no other science can be placed higher—the nature of knowledge can go no further?"

"i agree," he said.

"but to whom we should assign these studies, and how they should be assigned, are questions that remain to be considered."

"yes, clearly."

"you remember," i said, "how the rulers were chosen before?"

"certainly," he said.

"the same natures must still be chosen, and the preference again given to the most reliable and the bravest, and, if possible, to the best looking; and, having noble and generous temperaments, they should also have the natural gifts that'll facilitate their education."

"and what are these?"

"such gifts as sharpness and quick learning abilities; cause the mind more often gets exhausted from the severity of study than from the severity of gymnastics: the grind is more entirely the mind's own, and isn't shared with the body."

"very true," he replied.

"further, whoever we're looking for should have good memory, and be an untiring solid person who loves to grind in any line; or they'll never be able to handle the massive amount of bodily exercise and to go through all the intellectual discipline and study we require of them."

"certainly," he said; "they must have natural gifts."

"the mistake right now is that those who study philosophy have no calling, and this, as i was saying before, is why she's fallen into disrepute: her true children should take her by the hand and not bastards."

"what do you mean?"

"first, her follower shouldn't have a lame or halting grindset—i mean, they shouldn't be half industrious and half lazy: like, for example, when someone's a lover of gym and hunting, and all other bodily exercises, but hates rather than loves the labor of learning or listening or investigating. or the occupation they devote themselves to might be of an opposite kind, and they might have the other sort of lameness."

"certainly," he said.

"and regarding truth," i said, "isn't a soul equally to be considered halt and lame which hates voluntary falsehood and gets extremely pressed at herself and others when they lie, but is patient of involuntary falsehood, and doesn't mind wallowing like a pig in the mud of ignorance, and has no shame at being caught?"

"for sure."

"and, again, regarding temperance, courage, magnificence, and every other virtue, shouldn't we carefully distinguish between the true child and the bastard? cause where there's no discernment of such qualities states and individuals unconsciously mess up; and the state makes a ruler, and the individual a friend, of someone who, being defective in some part of virtue, is basically lame or a bastard."

"that's very true," he said.

"all these things, then, will have to be carefully considered by us; and if only those we introduce to this massive system of education and training are sound in body and mind, justice herself will have nothing to say against us, and we'll be the saviors of the constitution and the state; but, if our students are of another type, the reverse will happen, and we'll pour an even greater flood of roasts on philosophy than she has to endure right now."

"that wouldn't be creditable."

"definitely not," i said; "and yet perhaps, in turning jokes into seriousness i'm equally ridiculous."

"in what respect?"

"i'd forgotten," i said, "that we weren't serious, and spoke with too much heat. cause when i saw philosophy so undeservedly getting trampled by people i couldn't help feeling some type of way at the authors of her disgrace: and my anger made me too intense."

"really! i was listening, and didn't think so."

"but i, who am the speaker, felt that i was. and now let me remind you that, although in our former selection we chose old people, we can't do that here. solon was capping when he said that someone when they grow old can learn many things—cause they can no more learn much than they can run much; youth is the time for any extraordinary grind."

"of course."

"and, therefore, calculation and geometry and all the other basics of instruction, which are preparation for dialectic, should be presented to the mind in childhood; not, however, under any notion of forcing our education system."

"why not?"

"because a free person shouldn't be a slave in acquiring knowledge of any kind. bodily exercise, when compulsory, does no harm to the body; but knowledge acquired under compulsion gets no grip on the mind."

"very true."

"then, bestie," i said, "don't use force, but let early education be a sort of game; you'll then be better able to find out the natural inclination."
